Where lung MDT time goes: findings from eight teams

Evidence3 min readLast reviewed 21 November 2026

In 2023, Zasada and colleagues published a mixed-methods study in Cancer Medicine that observed every lung cancer MDT meeting in one UK cancer network for 12 weeks. That came to 8 teams and 96 meetings. The researchers recorded attendance and data on each case discussion with a validated MDT tool, then interviewed team members about what got in the way.

What they found

  • A treatment recommendation was agreed in 48% of case discussions.
  • The recommendation was deferred in 24%, most often because results were still awaited.
  • Meetings were quorate in 21% of observations. Palliative care was the specialty most often missing.
  • 37% of cases were repeat discussions held before a diagnosis had been made.
  • Fitness for treatment or performance status was available for 60% of cases, ranging from 30% to 75% by team.

The authors concluded that inefficiencies were present in every meeting they observed. Ways of working varied so much between teams that improvement would need an approach fitted to each one.

Why these numbers matter

A deferred case comes back next week. The same people re-read the same history and the patient waits another seven days for a plan. A case discussed without performance status rarely gets a recommendation that holds, because fitness is what separates surgery, radical radiotherapy, systemic therapy and best supportive care. A meeting that is not quorate cannot speak for the specialties that were absent.

What staff said

Interviews pointed to three themes: workforce shortages, a wish to rely less on the MDT for decisions made before diagnosis, and better access to key patient information. None of these is solved by a longer meeting.

What a board can fix first

The study does not prescribe one fix, and the authors argue for local solutions. These are the changes we would start with, because each one maps to a finding.

  1. Make results a condition of listing. If a case is waiting for a result that will change the decision, list it when the result is back. This goes straight at the 24% deferral rate.
  2. Record performance status at intake. Treat ECOG as part of registering the case. In OncoBoard, ECOG sits on the case record beside the AJCC stage group, so its absence is visible before the meeting starts.
  3. Know who is in the room before the case starts. In OncoBoard the Live Board Room shows which specialties are connected, and the chair can see whether three specialties have voted before ratifying.
  4. Separate pre-diagnosis questions from treatment decisions. Ask whether each pre-diagnosis question needs the full meeting or can be agreed against a local protocol. Our piece on NHS streamlining guidance covers how.
  5. Measure your own board. Count deferrals and their reasons for one month. Track turnaround from intake to consensus by board; Clinical Analytics shows it against a 72-hour target and exports it as CSV.

Limits of the study

The findings describe lung MDTs in one UK network over 12 weeks. Other tumour types and other health systems may look different. That is part of the authors' argument: measure your own meetings before deciding what to change.

In January 2020, NHS England and NHS Improvement published Streamlining Multi-Disciplinary Team Meetings: Guidance for Cancer Alliances. It gives teams a way to stop treating every case the same. Cases that fit an agreed standard of care can be managed against it, and the meeting's time goes to the cases that need several specialists at once.

What the guidance asks for

  • Standards of Care. Each Cancer Alliance agrees standards that set out the treatment a patient should expect for defined presentations.
  • Routine stratification. Teams apply those standards as a routine part of MDT meetings to sort cases. Cases that fit a standard are managed against it. Full discussion is reserved for complex cases.
  • Optional, then binding. Streamlining is not mandatory. A trust that chooses to introduce it must follow the guidance.

What has to be in place first

Streamlining moves a decision earlier in the week. That decision is only safe if the case is complete when it is made. In our experience these five things decide whether it works.

  1. Written standards per tumour site. Each standard names the presentation it covers and the treatment it recommends, and who signed it off.
  2. A triage step before the meeting. A named person, often the chair with the coordinator, decides for each case: standard of care or full discussion.
  3. A minimum dataset at triage. Histology, stage, performance status and any molecular result that would change the plan. In the 2023 study of eight lung MDTs, performance status was available for only 60% of cases discussed. Triage without it is guesswork.
  4. A record for streamlined cases too. A case managed against a standard still needs a documented outcome, with who confirmed it and when.
  5. A route back. A new result, a change in fitness or a patient's preference sends the case back to full discussion.

How OncoBoard supports it

  • The Patient Dossier puts histology, IHC, NGS results, ECOG and the AJCC stage group on one page, so triage works from a complete picture.

Questions to settle before you start

  • Who signs off the standards of care for each tumour site, and how often are they reviewed?
  • Who triages, by when, and what happens if the minimum dataset is incomplete?
  • How will patients and their referring teams be told about a streamlined decision?
  • How will you audit streamlined decisions against discussed ones?

RECIST 1.1 turns a follow-up scan into one of four words: complete response, partial response, stable disease or progressive disease. Those words often decide whether a patient stays on treatment. The board should agree the target lesions at baseline, because every later call depends on them.

Measurable or not

  • A non-nodal lesion is measurable if its longest diameter is at least 10 mm on CT with a slice thickness of 5 mm or less (at least 20 mm on a chest X-ray).
  • A lymph node is measurable as a target if its short axis is at least 15 mm.
  • A node of 10 mm to under 15 mm short axis is pathological but non-measurable. Record it as a non-target lesion.
  • A node under 10 mm short axis is considered non-pathological.

Choosing target lesions

Choose up to five target lesions in total, with a maximum of two per organ. Pick the largest lesions that can be measured reproducibly. A lesion that is hard to see on the next scan makes a poor target, however large it is today. Everything else is recorded as non-target.

The sum of diameters adds the longest diameter of each non-nodal target and the short axis of each nodal target. Use the same imaging technique at every time point.

Response categories

  • Complete response: all target lesions have disappeared, and every pathological node has a short axis under 10 mm.
  • Partial response: the sum has fallen by at least 30% from the baseline sum.
  • Progressive disease: the sum has risen by at least 20% from the smallest sum on study (the nadir), with an absolute increase of at least 5 mm. A new lesion is also progression.
  • Stable disease: neither enough shrinkage for partial response nor enough growth for progression.

A worked example

ONC-2026-4102 has three target lesions at baseline: a right upper lobe mass, a liver segment VI lesion and a subcarinal node. All values are in millimetres.

Time pointRUL massLiver VINode (short axis)SumResponse
Baseline24.518.016.058.5Baseline
Week 1215.011.09.035.0Partial response, −40.2% from baseline
Week 2413.010.08.031.0Partial response, −47.0%; new nadir
Week 3617.013.09.039.0Progressive disease, +25.8% and +8.0 mm from nadir

At week 36 the sum is still 33.3% below baseline. Compared with baseline it looks like a partial response. Compared with the nadir of 31.0 mm it has grown by 8.0 mm, which is 25.8%. Both thresholds for progression are met, so the response is progressive disease.

Traps worth naming at the board

  • Progression is measured from the nadir. Partial response is measured from baseline. Mixing them up is the commonest error.
  • Nodes stay in the sum. A target node that shrinks below 10 mm is still recorded at its actual short axis. The sum does not fall to zero.
  • Non-target lesions count. Unequivocal progression of non-target disease is progression, even when the target sum is stable.
  • Change of technique. A different slice thickness or modality can create change that is not real.

In OncoBoard

Measurements are calibrated in DICOM Studio and saved to the case as target or non-target lesions. The RECIST summary shows the sum and response, so the board discusses the same numbers the radiologist measured.

The MDT coordinator decides whether a board runs on time. Most of that work happens before anyone joins the meeting. This checklist covers one 60-minute board from two days out to the hour after it ends.

T−48 hours: build the list

  • Confirm every case is registered with MRN, initials, board and urgency. Use initials only. Full names have no place on a board list.
  • Check the essentials for each case: histology, TNM with a resolved AJCC stage group, ECOG, and any molecular result the decision depends on.
  • Move cases still waiting on a result that will change the plan to the next board. A case listed without it is likely to be deferred.
  • Flag Urgent cases first. In OncoBoard, urgent cases are tracked against a 72-hour turnaround target.

T−24 hours: set the agenda

  • Queue cases in the Board Schedule in the order the board will take them. Put cases that need a specialist with a hard stop early.
  • Budget the hour. Eight cases at seven minutes each is 56 minutes, leaving four minutes for overruns. If the list is longer, the board will rush the last cases.
  • Confirm which specialties will attend. A treatment decision without the right specialties is likely to come back next week.
  • Tell the presenting radiologist and pathologist which cases they own.
  • Send attendees the list in the order it will run, so nobody waits through cases they are not needed for.

The morning of the board

  • Check that late results have arrived and moved the case into place in the dossier.
  • Open the session in good time so presenters can check their studies load.

During the meeting

  • Press Go Live and keep the case timer visible. Call time at the budget, kindly.
  • Make sure each specialty votes on the record. In OncoBoard, quorum is met when three specialties have voted.
  • Capture deferrals with a reason and a date. A deferral without a reason tends to repeat.
  • Record the recommendation in the room. A decision written up the next day loses the reasoning and any dissent.

Within the hour after

  • Check every discussed case has a ratified recommendation or a recorded deferral.
  • Export the consensus report for each case and file it in the EHR.
  • End the session so the record closes cleanly.

Every month

  • Review turnaround from intake to consensus by board in Clinical Analytics, and export it as CSV for the governance meeting.
  • Count deferrals and their reasons. Fix the commonest one first.
  • Check that decisions were carried out. In one prospective breast cancer study, 20 of 289 MDT decisions (6.9%) were not implemented.

A tumour board has too much to read and too little time to write. That makes it a good place for AI to help. It is also a place where a confident error reaches a patient. These are the principles we hold OncoBoard's AI to, and the questions we would ask of any system.

Where AI helps

  • Synthesis before the meeting. A one-page brief of staging, pathology, molecular drivers and open questions saves every member from re-reading the record.
  • Drafting minutes. Turning votes and discussion into minutes is slow work. A draft the chair edits is faster than a blank page.
  • A second look. A structured read of the current DICOM slice can prompt the radiologist to check something. It is a prompt. The radiologist still reads the study.
  • Answering questions about the case. Plain-language questions answered from the dossier in front of the board.

Seven principles

  1. AI prepares. People decide. No AI output becomes a recommendation until a clinician makes it one.
  2. No votes, no sign-off, no edits. An assistant cannot cast a specialty vote, ratify a consensus or change the record.
  3. Label everything. Every output is marked as AI decision support, so nobody mistakes a draft for a decision.
  4. End with a disclaimer. Every output reminds the reader to verify it against source records.
  5. Ground it in the case. Answers should come from the patient's own dossier, not from a generic prompt.
  6. Verify anything that would change management. If an answer cites a trial, check the eligibility. For example, LAURA enrolled patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC, an EGFR exon 19 deletion or L858R mutation, and no progression after chemoradiation. Median progression-free survival was 39.1 months with osimertinib against 5.6 months with placebo. Whether a given patient fits is the board's call.
  7. Keep identifiable data out. Cases carry an MRN and initials. A full name should never reach a model.

How OncoBoard applies them

OncoBoard has four assistants: case synthesis, DICOM slice read, consensus minutes and case questions. They run on the Google Gemini API, called from the server, and only when a clinician triggers one. Each output carries the AI Decision Support label and a disclaimer. Voting, ratifying and editing the record are clinician actions only.

Questions to ask any vendor

  • What data leaves your environment, and to which provider?
  • Who can trigger the AI, and is anything generated without a person asking?
  • How is AI output labelled on screen and in exported reports?
  • Can AI output change the clinical record without a person accepting it?
  • How are clinicians expected to verify an answer before acting on it?

The fifth edition of the WHO Classification of Tumours of the Central Nervous System, known as WHO CNS5, was published in 2021. It made molecular results part of the diagnosis for many brain tumours. For board members outside neuro-oncology, the practical effect is simple: a histology report on its own is often not a final diagnosis any more.

What changed

  • Integrated diagnosis. The diagnosis combines histology, grade and molecular findings into one name.
  • Grades within types. Tumours are graded within each tumour type, written as CNS WHO grade in Arabic numerals.
  • NOS and NEC. "Not otherwise specified" means the molecular testing needed was not done or did not work. "Not elsewhere classified" means it was done, and the tumour does not fit a defined type.

For the board, the day a diagnosis becomes final can be the day the molecular result arrives. Build that wait into the pathway, and list the case once the integrated diagnosis is complete unless the decision cannot wait.

The three adult-type diffuse gliomas

  • Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant: CNS WHO grade 2, 3 or 4. Homozygous deletion of CDKN2A/B makes it grade 4, even without necrosis or microvascular proliferation.
  • Oligodendroglioma, IDH-mutant and 1p/19q-codeleted: grade 2 or 3. It needs both the IDH mutation and the 1p/19q codeletion.
  • Glioblastoma, IDH-wildtype: grade 4. The name now applies only to IDH-wildtype tumours. An IDH-wildtype, H3-wildtype diffuse astrocytic glioma counts as glioblastoma without necrosis or microvascular proliferation if it has a TERT promoter mutation, EGFR amplification, or gain of chromosome 7 with loss of chromosome 10.

Where MGMT fits

MGMT promoter methylation predicts benefit from temozolomide. It is not a diagnostic criterion. It belongs in the treatment discussion, not the diagnosis line.

Midline tumours

Diffuse midline glioma, H3 K27-altered is classed as a paediatric-type diffuse glioma, though it also occurs in adults. If the report mentions H3 K27, the board is discussing a different entity from the adult-type gliomas above.

What to ask the pathologist

  • What is the IDH status, and how was it tested?
  • If IDH-mutant: is 1p/19q codeleted, and is CDKN2A/B deleted?
  • If IDH-wildtype: what are the TERT promoter, EGFR and chromosome 7/10 results?
  • What is the MGMT promoter methylation status?
  • Is any part of the diagnosis NOS because testing is still pending?

If the answer to the last question is yes, consider whether the case should wait for the result before a treatment decision.
